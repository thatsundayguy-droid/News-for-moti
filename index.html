CFA Corner
Duration and the Bond Math Behind This Yield Spike
With the US 10-year at 5.26%, understanding Modified Duration helps explain why bond portfolios are suffering -- and how to think about the interest-rate risk in your equity holdings too.
CFA Prep Desk

Duration is one of the most tested -- and most misunderstood -- concepts in fixed income. At its simplest, Macaulay Duration is the weighted average time to receive a bond's cash flows, measured in years. Modified Duration converts that into a price-sensitivity number: how much does a bond's price change for a 1% (100 basis point) move in yields?

Modified Duration = Macaulay Duration / (1 + y/m)

% Price Change ≈ −Modified Duration × Δy

Where y is the yield to maturity and m is the number of coupon periods per year. A negative sign means bond prices fall when yields rise -- the core inverse relationship.

A 10-year zero-coupon bond has a Modified Duration near 9.5 at 5% yield. If yields rise 25 basis points (from 5% to 5.25%), the price falls approximately 9.5 × 0.25% = 2.375%. This is exactly what the current environment is doing to long-duration bond portfolios globally -- including India's long-dated G-Sec holdings at insurance companies and pension funds.

Why Duration Matters for Equity Investors Too

Many equity investors dismiss duration as a "bonds thing," but growth stocks are long-duration assets. A company whose cash flows arrive mostly 10--15 years in the future behaves like a long-duration bond: its present value is exquisitely sensitive to the discount rate. When the US 10-year yield rises from 4% to 5.26%, the discount rate for future cash flows rises substantially -- mechanically compressing the present value of growth stocks.

IT and growth stocks -- long-duration by nature -- face a structural headwind until yields peak and plateau.

This is one reason Indian IT stocks have been relatively weak even as earnings remain solid. Nifty IT's comparative resilience (-0.26% vs. PSU Banks' -3.24%) masks a larger point: IT would be rallying much harder in a lower-yield world. The same applies to any stock with a premium P/E multiple -- the denominator in the discount rate has risen sharply, compressing what investors will pay for distant earnings.

Quick Concept Check:
  • Coupon bond: Duration < Maturity (intermediate cash flows shorten weighted average)
  • Zero-coupon bond: Duration = Maturity exactly
  • Higher coupon rate → lower duration (more early cash flows)
  • Callable bonds: Duration falls as yields drop (call option gets more valuable)
